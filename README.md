# Moon Base

A browser-based 3D construction and management game about building a base at the lunar south pole, where nothing appears by magic. See the [spec](https://github.com/barnabyg/moonBase/issues/1) for what the game will be and its eight phases, [`GLOSSARY.md`](GLOSSARY.md) for the game's language and [`docs/adr/`](docs/adr/) for the design decisions.

Desktop Firefox is the only supported browser.

## Play it

You need [Node.js](https://nodejs.org/) 24 or later. Double-click `run.bat` in the repository's root folder (the one containing `package.json`), or run it from PowerShell in any folder:

```powershell
C:\docs\git\moonBase\run.bat
```

It installs or updates the dependencies when needed, starts the game at <http://localhost:5173> and opens it in Firefox (or your default browser if Firefox isn't installed in the usual place). Stop it by closing its window or pressing `Ctrl+C` in it.

Without the launcher, run `npm.cmd ci && npm.cmd run dev` from the repository's root folder and open <http://localhost:5173> in Firefox.

The bar across the top of the screen holds the game clock, the game speed, "Skip to next event", the Funding and Science balances with the Launch manifest, Research Tasks and Tech Tree buttons, and saving and loading. Below it, the status panel on the left shows how the Mission Director is keeping, the base's power and stock, the Lander and Expeditions, and the Research Task running, above the build menu; the Base panel on the right lists the Structures, Robots, Machines and Rovers with their Condition, between the Work panel and the notification list. Each section of the status and Base panels folds away when you click its title, and stays folded on your next visit; a dot by a folded title means something in it needs attention. Hover over a self-sufficiency figure or the time left in the Lunation for the whole story. In the game, drag with the left mouse button to pan the camera, drag with the right button to rotate it, and use the wheel to zoom from the whole base map down to a close-up of the Robot. "Skip to next event" jumps straight to the next notable moment, such as Night falling, a Construction being commissioned or a warning; each one also appears in the notification list at the bottom right, which never pauses the game. To plan a Construction, pick a Structure in the build menu at the bottom left, move its translucent preview over the map and click to place it (right-click or Esc cancels); the Robot then carries it out by itself, and the Work panel at the top right shows each stage. For Site Preparation the Robot drives the excavator to the site and activates it, and the excavator grades the ground by itself while the Robot is free for other work. Every Construction after the first that uses power also has the Robot lay power cable from the nearest powered Structure (one on a network with a solar array or battery bank); only Structures joined by cable share power. If a Structure that uses power is left without any power source, for example when the solar array it was cabled from is dismantled or because it was built before there was one, the Robot lays it a new cable from the nearest powered Structure, in plan order, as soon as there is one. Solar arrays generate only in Daylight, battery banks store any surplus and cover any shortfall, and the status panel shows generation, use, stored energy and when stored power will run out. Light masts switch on by themselves at Night and light up the ground around them, drawing power only while they're on; they only help you see, as work carries on in the dark without them. The Robot works on Constructions and dismantling in the order you planned them. The base's stock of Components is kept at a stockpile on the ground beside the Lander, where the Robot loads a Construction's Components and unloads those recovered by dismantling; nothing can be built over it. A Construction whose Components aren't in the base's stock waits, with the reason shown in the Work panel, and the Robot gets on with the next one. "Cancel" in the Work panel removes an unfinished Construction at once and puts its unused Components (those not yet assembled) back in the base's stock. "Dismantle" beside a commissioned Structure in the Base panel has the Robot take it apart and carry its Components back to the stockpile; a share of them, set in the data catalogue, is damaged beyond reuse. The Structure keeps working until the Robot starts taking it apart. The Mission Director starts aboard the Lander, and the starting stock holds an inflatable Habitat kit: once the Habitat is commissioned, "Move in" beside it in the Base panel moves the Mission Director in at once, for good, as long as the Habitat has power. On the map they climb down the Lander's ladder and walk to the Habitat's airlock; once there, its portholes light up and the mission flag is raised over it, high enough to show above Regolith shielding. The status panel then shows the Lander as free to fly, and the Habitat they live in can't be dismantled. The base starts with about 30 Earth Days of oxygen and water. The Lander keeps them safe indefinitely, but once the Mission Director lives in the Habitat they use some every Earth Day, and the status panel shows how much of each is left and when it will run out. Warnings come when 7, 3 and 1 Earth Days of a stock are left (the data catalogue sets these). If either runs out, the Mission Director dies at once and the Campaign is over: the game-over screen says how they died, and "Start a new Campaign" starts again from the landing (with the same seed, if the address gives one). The Habitat needs power too. When a power network can't cover everything, the Habitat is supplied first and the other Structures on it, such as Light Masts and battery banks, lose power before it does, always in the order they were commissioned. The Habitat only works with all the power it draws: if the network can cover just part of it, the Habitat has no power. While the Mission Director lives in the Habitat, the status panel shows when it will lose power, and warnings come 24, 6 and 1 hours before (the data catalogue sets these); moving in with less time left than a warning level gives that warning at once. If it loses power, the Mission Director dies at once; an unpowered Habitat with nobody in it kills nobody. With only the starting battery bank, the Habitat can't get through a whole Night. The Mission Director also absorbs a Radiation Dose: none aboard the Lander, and a steady rate in the Habitat while it has no shielding. The status panel shows the dose so far against the limit, how fast it is rising and when it will reach the limit; warnings come 7, 3 and 1 Earth Days before, and reaching the limit kills the Mission Director (the data catalogue sets the rates, the limit and the warnings). To cut the dose rate, pick Regolith shielding in the build menu and place it on a commissioned Habitat; it's the only thing that may overlap another Structure, and anywhere else it is refused with the reason. The excavator grades the ground round the Habitat, then the Robot drives the regolith printer (which landed beside the excavator) to the Habitat and activates it, and the printer builds the shielding up over the Habitat by itself, layer by layer, from Regolith taken straight from the ground, so it needs no Components and no cable. Once the shielding is commissioned, the Base panel shows the Habitat as shielded and the dose rate there falls to a tenth. A shielded Habitat can't be dismantled until its shielding has been. Every so often, at random (but the same for the same seed and choices), a Solar Particle Event comes: a warning arrives 4 hours ahead, the status panel shows it while it is coming and under way, and "Skip to next event" stops at its warning, its start and its end. While it lasts, the dose rate where the Mission Director lives is ten times as high, and the forecasts and warnings take that into account: aboard the Lander it stays zero, a shielded Habitat keeps it low, and in an unshielded Habitat one Solar Particle Event can take half the dose limit (the data catalogue sets how often they come, how long they last, the warning time and the multiplier). From its warning on, the time until the limit follows the Solar Particle Event through to its end.

The Mission Director starts the Campaign with the Programme Grant, and the top bar shows the Funding balance. "Launch manifest" beside it opens a screen where you choose what the next Launch brings from Earth: any Component whose Design is unlocked, plus oxygen and water in set amounts, each with a mass and a price from the data catalogue. The screen shows the manifest's total Payload Mass against the Launch's limit, and its cost (the Launch fee plus each item's price, or nothing while the manifest is empty) against the Funding balance. An item that would take the manifest over either limit is refused with the reason. The game runs on while the screen is open. The status panel also lists the Components in the base's stock.

Once the Mission Director has moved into a Habitat, "Send the Lander" beside the Lander in the status panel sends it on its round trip: it takes off on a plume of dust, flies to Earth (3 Earth Days), spends 4 Earth Days in turnaround there and flies back (3 Earth Days) to land on the same spot (the data catalogue sets each leg). The status panel shows which leg it is on and when it next arrives or leaves, and "Skip to next event" stops as it takes off, reaches Earth, leaves Earth and lands. While the Mission Director still lives aboard, sending it is refused with the reason. The base's stock and the Robot's work carry on while it is away. You can go on changing the Launch manifest until the Lander leaves Earth. Then the manifest locks, and its cost (the Launch fee plus the item prices, or nothing if it is empty) comes off the Funding. When the Lander lands, its Components join the base's stock at the stockpile, so a Construction waiting for them goes ahead, and its oxygen and water join the life support stocks. A new, empty manifest then starts. Running out of Funding never ends the Campaign by itself.

Every take-off and landing blasts Plume Ejecta over the ground round the Lander, wearing down the Condition of every Structure with any of its footprint within 30 m, by 20 percentage points each time (the data catalogue sets both). Every commissioned Structure starts at 100%, shown beside it in the Base panel's list of Structures, and a notification says what was worn down. At 0% a Structure is Broken: it stays on the map but stops working, so a Broken solar array generates nothing, a Broken battery bank loses its stored energy and neither stores nor supplies power, a Broken Light Mast gives no light, and a Broken Habitat loses its power and its shielding, which kills the Mission Director if they live in it. Broken Regolith shielding no longer shields its Habitat. While you place a Structure, the build menu shows the Plume Ejecta danger zone round the Lander and warns if the Structure would stand in it; placing it there is allowed.

The fix is a landing pad, which you can build anywhere clear of other Structures. Its pad panels and beacon aren't in the starting stock, so a Launch has to bring them first. Once it is commissioned, the Lander's next landing, and every take-off and landing after that, is on the pad, with no Plume Ejecta, and the danger zone no longer shows while you place a Structure (a take-off from the landing site before then still blasts it). Oxygen and water join the life support stocks as the Lander lands on the pad, but its Components stay aboard: an unloading Work Order has the Robot walk to the Lander and carry them to the stockpile, taking its turn among the other Work Orders in plan order, and the Work panel shows it. They only join the base's stock, and a Construction waiting for them only goes ahead, once they are there, and the Lander can't leave until the Robot has taken them off. Nothing can be built over the Lander on its pad, and the landing site is always kept clear. The pad can't be dismantled while the Lander stands on it; once it is dismantled, or if it is Broken, the Lander lands at the landing site again, with Plume Ejecta. The Habitat can't get through a Night on the starting battery bank, and the Lander can't be back in time if it leaves in the first Daylight, so the Mission Director is safest waiting aboard until the next Daylight.

Maintenance repairs what wears down. Once a commissioned Structure's Condition falls below 70%, or it is Broken, a Maintenance Work Order is raised for it, one at a time for each Structure, and the Base panel shows it as awaiting Maintenance, then undergoing it. The Robot takes Maintenance ahead of every other Work Order, in the order it was raised, once it has finished the Work Order it is on. It loads spare Components at the stockpile, carries them to the Structure and works on it for 2 hours, bringing its Condition back to 100%, so a Broken Structure works again; it uses one spare Component for every 20 percentage points of Condition restored, or part of one (the data catalogue sets the threshold, the times and the rate). The Work panel lists Maintenance first and shows each step, and a notification says when it is done. The starting stock holds 6 spare Components, and a Launch can bring more. If the base's stock has too few, the Maintenance Work Order waits, the Work panel says how many more it needs, and the Robot gets on with other Work Orders meanwhile. A Structure that is to be dismantled is not maintained.

Dust wears everything down too. Every commissioned Structure loses Condition for each hour it operates, at a rate the data catalogue sets for its type (a solar array loses 0.05 percentage points an hour, Regolith shielding none). A Structure operates while it isn't Broken and, if it has anything to do with power, while it has power, so a solar array with no battery bank doesn't wear at Night; one with nothing to do with power, such as a landing pad, always operates. The Base panel shows each Structure's Condition, rounded to the whole percent, and a notification says when one needs Maintenance ("Skip to next event" stops there). Below 50% Condition (the data catalogue sets the threshold and the curve), a Structure works less well, in step with its Condition: a solar array generates less, a battery bank stores less and loses any energy above what it can now hold, and a Light Mast lights a smaller circle. The Base panel shows how well such a Structure is working, and the power figures and forecasts follow the reduced output. A Habitat, Regolith shielding and a landing pad work fully until they are Broken. Maintenance brings back full performance.

Dust also settles on solar arrays: each one's Dust coating builds up from 0% by 0.1 percentage points for every hour of Daylight, and none at Night, cutting its output by its share of 60% (a full coating takes away 60%), on top of any drop from low Condition. The Base panel shows each array's Dust coating, and the power figures and forecasts follow it. Once a coating passes 30%, a Maintenance Work Order is raised to clean the array, with the same priority as other Maintenance: the Robot walks straight to the array, with no spare Components, and spends an hour cleaning it back to 0% (the data catalogue sets the rate, the share, the threshold and the time). An array due both for cleaning and for its Condition is done on one visit: the Robot restores its Condition, then cleans it. Short of the spare Components for its Condition, it is cleaned anyway, and its Condition waits for the spares.

Machines have a Condition too, shown with what each is doing in the Base panel's list of Machines. Dust wears a Machine down for each hour it works by itself or a Robot drives it (the excavator 0.4 percentage points an hour working and 0.2 driven), and not while it stands idle. Below 50% Condition it works and drives more slowly, in step with its Condition (the data catalogue sets the rates, the threshold and the curve). At 0% a Machine is Broken: it stops where it stands, with a notification, and gives up the stage of Construction it was on, which waits, with the reason in the Work panel, until another Machine of its type is free or the Broken one has been maintained. Machines get Maintenance Work Orders like Structures, at the same threshold and priority, using spare Components the same way: the Robot loads them at the stockpile and walks to the Machine. A Machine carrying out a stage of Construction is maintained once it is idle again, unless it is Broken, and isn't given new work while it is being maintained.

The cold of Night wears down Machines left without power, by 0.2 percentage points an hour on top of any Dust wear. A Machine standing within 3 m of a commissioned Structure on a power network with a solar array or battery bank, one that isn't Broken, is plugged in: at Night its heater draws 0.2 kW from that network, after every Structure on it, so the Habitat is still supplied first, and the power figures and forecasts count it. A Machine that isn't plugged in, or whose network can't supply its heater, is unpowered, and the Base panel shows which Machines are plugged in and powered. Six hours before Night falls, the Robot starts parking each idle Machine that isn't plugged in: it walks to the Machine and drives it to a free spot beside the nearest powered Structure, as long as there is one. During Night it does the same whenever a Machine becomes idle away from power; a Machine taken away to work at Night is unplugged while it is away, and feels the cold. Parking comes after Maintenance and before every other Work Order, and the Work panel shows it (the data catalogue sets the rates, the heater power, the reach and the lead time). Heaters on top of the Habitat make the starting battery bank run out sooner.

The Robot has a Condition too, shown with what it is doing in the Base panel's list of Robots. Dust wears it down by 0.1 percentage points for each hour it is doing anything but standing idle, and below 50% it walks and works more slowly, falling off gently at first. Below 10% it stops what it is doing, with a notification, and repairs itself ahead of everything else: it collects spare Components at the stockpile, then goes to wherever the Mission Director lives, the Habitat or, while they live aboard it, the Lander, and spends 3 hours repairing itself there, back to 100%, using spare Components at the Maintenance rate. It keeps whatever it was carrying, and takes up the Work Order it was on again afterwards. With too few spare Components it makes a makeshift repair instead, to 30%, and carries on working, slowly. Its Condition never falls below 1%: the Robot is never Broken (the data catalogue sets the rate, the curve, the thresholds, the time and the levels).

Micrometeorites strike at random, without warning, every 4 to 20 Earth Days (but the same for the same seed and choices), each hitting one commissioned Structure, Machine or the Robot, chosen at random, and taking 5 to 25 percentage points off its Condition (the data catalogue sets both ranges). A notification names what was hit and the Condition it was left with. A strike can leave a Structure or Machine Broken, with the usual consequences, but the Robot's Condition stops at its floor. The damage raises Maintenance, or the Robot's self-repair, through the usual thresholds.

The status panel shows the spare Components in stock and how long they will last, as things stand: from how fast everything is wearing down now (Dust on what is operating, the cold on Machines unpowered at Night, the Robot's work and the average damage Micrometeorites do), turned into spare Components at the Maintenance rate, after the spares that Maintenance already raised, or the Robot's repair of itself, will take. With nothing wearing down, they last indefinitely. Warnings come as the time left falls to 7, 3 and 1 Earth Days (the data catalogue sets these), and "Skip to next event" stops at them. The rate jumps as the Robot and the Machines start and stop work, so each warning is given once, until more spare Components arrive in the base's stock, as when a Launch brings them, or the forecast is back above twice the highest level.

A Rover arrives in the starting cargo, parked near the Lander, and the Base panel lists it with its Condition. It goes on no Expeditions yet, but it wears down like a Machine: Micrometeorites can strike it, the cold of Night wears it down by 0.2 percentage points an hour while it is unpowered, and the Robot parks it beside the nearest powered Structure before Night, after the Machines, so that it plugs in and its 0.2 kW heater draws from that network. Driving wears it down by Dust too (0.1 percentage points an hour). Below the Maintenance threshold, or Broken, it gets a Maintenance Work Order like a Machine's, using spare Components the same way, and the spare Components forecast counts its wear. A Broken Rover can't be driven until it has been maintained. Nothing can be built over it (the data catalogue sets its size, speed, heater and rates of wear).

The base also keeps a stock of bulk resources at the stockpile, measured in kilograms and shown in the status panel; Regolith is the first. Whenever the base has less than 1000 kg of Regolith and the excavator is free, the Robot sets it digging, but only when it has no other Work Order to carry out: it walks to the excavator, drives it to clear ground beside the stockpile and activates it, and the excavator digs by itself, 50 kg a game-hour (less below 50% Condition), until the stock reaches the level. Digging always gives way: a Construction's Site Preparation takes the excavator off digging as soon as it needs it, as do Maintenance and parking before Night, and digging carries on afterwards while the stock is still short. No digging starts while Machines are being parked for Night, if there is a powered Structure to park beside. The excavator wears down by Dust for each hour it digs, as it does working on a site, and a Broken excavator doesn't dig (the data catalogue sets the level and the rate).

"Expeditions" beside the Expedition line in the status panel opens the Expeditions screen, where "Start an Expedition" sends the Robot off the map in the Rover. It is refused, with the reason, while one is already under way, while the Rover is Broken, or once the Campaign has ended. The Robot finishes the Work Order it holds, then walks to the Rover and drives it off the nearest map edge; it is away for 3 Earth Days, then drives back to the stockpile and unloads 200 kg of water ice into the base's bulk resources (the data catalogue sets the time, the amount and the unloading time). While it is away, every Work Order waits, including Maintenance and parking before Night, and the Work panel and the Base panel say the Robot is on an Expedition; it doesn't repair itself until it is back. The Rover wears down by Dust for every hour of the Expedition, as it is driven out, away and back, but never below 1%, so that it always comes back; the cold and Micrometeorites pass both of them by while they are away. Leaving and coming back are notifications, "Skip to next event" stops at both, and the Expeditions screen and the status panel show when it will be back. Nothing uses the ice yet.

Research Tasks earn Science, which the top bar shows beside the Funding. "Research Tasks" beside it opens the Research Tasks screen, which lists every Research Task with its length and the Science it pays: from a 6-hour Regolith sample analysis paying 5 Science to a 7-Earth-Day low-frequency radio survey paying 60 (the data catalogue sets them all; the short ones pay more Science an hour, the long ones run unattended). "Start" beside one starts it. Only one runs at a time, and each can be completed only once, so starting another while one runs, or one already completed, is refused with the reason. A running Research Task costs only game time: it needs no Robot, power or Components, and carries on through Night, while the Lander is away and while the Robot is busy or away on an Expedition. The screen and the Research section of the status panel show which one is running and the time left, and the screen marks those completed. When it is complete, its Science is added to the base's, a notification names it and the Science it earned, and "Skip to next event" stops there. The game runs on while the screen is open. A few Research Tasks need a Design unlocked first; until then the screen leaves them out, and starting one is refused with the Design it needs.

Science unlocks Designs in the Tech Tree. Everything that can be built or shipped today belongs to a starting Design, unlocked when the Campaign begins; a few more, such as Large battery banks (25 Science, after Battery banks) or a Pressurised Rover (55 Science, after Large battery banks and the Inflatable Habitat), cost Science and need other Designs first (the data catalogue sets them all). "Tech Tree" beside the Science balance opens the Tech Tree screen, which shows every Design by category (Power, Construction, Protection, Life Support, Industry and Mobility) with its cost, the Designs it needs first, and whether it is unlocked, can be unlocked now, or is out of reach, saying which Designs it still needs or how much more Science. "Unlock" beside one takes its cost off the Science and unlocks it at once, and a notification names it. Unlocking is refused, with the reason, when the Design is already unlocked, when a Design it needs isn't unlocked yet, or when there isn't enough Science. Unlocking a Design adds nothing to the base: it is permission to have something, not the thing itself. The game runs on while the screen is open.

Designs control what can be shipped and built. Every Launch item and Structure type belongs to one Design in the data catalogue, and one whose Design isn't unlocked is left off the Launch manifest screen and the build menu; it appears in both as soon as its Design is unlocked. Putting one on the Launch manifest, or planning one, is refused with the reason, which names the Design needed. Unlocking a Design still adds nothing to the base: its Components have to be shipped, and its Structure built, like any other. Components already in the base's stock or on their way, and Constructions already planned, are never affected; only shipping and planning are gated.

The large battery bank is the first Structure behind a Design that isn't a starting one: Large battery banks. It stores 240 kWh, four times a battery bank's 60, and its heaters draw 0.4 kW (a battery bank's draw 0.25). Its 4 large battery modules (170 kg, $10M each) and large battery cabinet (160 kg, $5M) aren't in the starting stock, so a Launch has to bring them: 840 kg and $45M in all, about half the mass and price of a battery bank for each kWh it stores. It stands on a 3 m footprint, takes about twice as long to build as a battery bank, and wears down by Dust at the same rate.

The extraction plant is the first Plant: a Structure that runs a recipe by itself once it is commissioned. You plan it from the build menu like any other Structure; its extraction reactor and oxygen separator aren't in the starting stock, so a Launch has to bring them (1000 kg between them), and the Robot lays it a cable. It turns 4 kg of Regolith a game-hour into oxygen, which joins the oxygen stock, and metals and silicon, which join the bulk resources (the vacuum fabrication plant turns the silicon into wafers, and the circuit board plant uses the metals). It has two recipes: running for oxygen, its default, it makes 0.15 kg of oxygen, 0.04 kg of metals and 0.02 kg of silicon; running for metals and silicon, it makes 0.6 kg of metals, 0.4 kg of silicon and only 0.01 kg of oxygen. It runs only while there is Regolith in stock, it has power and it isn't Broken, and the Base panel says which of these has stopped it. It draws 4 kW only while it runs, and it is supplied after everything else on its network, from power that nothing else uses and no battery bank can store, so it can never take power from the Habitat or anything else: in practice it runs in Daylight once the battery banks are full, and stops at Night. Below 50% Condition it runs more slowly, in step with its Condition, it wears down by Dust for each hour it runs, and Maintenance, Micrometeorites, Plume Ejecta and dismantling apply to it as to any Structure (the data catalogue sets the recipes, the power and the rates). The oxygen forecast follows what the base makes less what the Mission Director uses: while production outruns use, the status panel shows the stock rising instead of when it runs out, and no warnings come.

A commissioned Plant with more than one recipe, such as the extraction plant, has a recipe list beside it among the Structures in the Base panel, showing the one it runs, and choosing another there switches it from the next game-minute. It is a setting, not a Construction, so the Robot does no work for it. It is refused, with the reason, for a recipe the Plant doesn't have, for a Structure that isn't a commissioned Plant, or once the Campaign has ended. The forecasts and self-sufficiency follow the recipe each Plant runs: switched to metals and silicon, an extraction plant makes too little oxygen for the Mission Director, so the oxygen forecast turns to when it runs out. It runs only in Daylight, so keeping the Mission Director self-sufficient in oxygen takes running for oxygen about 30% of the Daylight, and the silicon it makes in the rest falls short of what a vacuum fabrication plant takes: one extraction plant can't do both, so you switch between the recipes as the stocks need, or import a second extraction plant.

The water-processing plant is the second Plant, and works the same way: its ice melter and water purifier come by Launch (400 kg between them), and once it is commissioned it turns 1 kg of water ice a game-hour, from what Expeditions bring back, into 0.9 kg of water for the water stock, drawing 2 kW only while it runs. It stops when the ice runs out, it has no power to spare or it is Broken, and the Base panel says which. The water forecast follows what the base makes less what the Mission Director uses, as the oxygen forecast does, and the status panel shows, beside any bulk resource that is falling, roughly how long it will last at the current rate, such as how long the ice will keep the plant running.

The vacuum fabrication plant is the third Plant, and works the same way: its crystal furnace and wafer saw come by Launch (750 kg between them, leaving room on the same Launch for a second solar array), and once it is commissioned it turns 0.35 kg of silicon a game-hour into 0.3 kg of wafers, a new bulk resource shown in the status panel after the silicon, which the base sells as an Export or turns into circuit boards. That is a little less silicon than an extraction plant makes running for metals and silicon, so the two running together turn Regolith into wafers without the silicon running out. It draws 6 kW only while it runs: the starting solar array can spare that on its own, but not as well as the extraction plant's 4 kW, so running both takes a second solar array. It stops when the silicon runs out, it has no power to spare or it is Broken, and the Base panel says which.

The circuit board plant is the fourth Plant, and works the same way: its circuit etcher and reflow oven come by Launch (600 kg between them), and once it is commissioned it turns 0.25 kg of wafers and 0.25 kg of metals a game-hour into 0.4 kg of circuit boards, a new bulk resource shown in the status panel after the wafers, which the base sells as an Export. That is a little less in wafers than a vacuum fabrication plant makes, and well within the metals an extraction plant makes running for metals and silicon, so the three running together turn Regolith into circuit boards without either input running out. It draws 3 kW only while it runs, so the three Plants together, at 13 kW, still run on what two solar arrays can spare. It runs only while there are both wafers and metals in stock, it has power and it isn't Broken; when it is out of wafers or out of metals, the Base panel names which.

Wafers and circuit boards are the Exports: the Lander carries them to Earth, where they are sold for Funding. Below the items on the Launch manifest screen, the Exports section lists each Export with what the base has in stock, its price per kilogram and the kilograms to load, which you type in, with their total mass against the Lander's Return Mass of 100 kg and what they will sell for (wafers fetch $1.5M a kg, so a full Return Mass pays for a Launch's fee; circuit boards fetch $3M a kg, so a full Return Mass of them pays for the fee and as much again, and wafers earn over three times as much made into circuit boards as sold as they are; the data catalogue sets the Return Mass, the Exports and their prices). An amount that would take the total over the Return Mass, or is more than the base has in stock, is refused with the reason, and so is any change while the Lander is away. At the landing site, beside the stockpile, the Exports stay in the base's stock until the Lander takes off, then go aboard (or as much of each as is left, if the stock has fallen since). On a landing pad, the Robot carries them instead: setting them raises a loading Work Order, in plan order after every plan made before it, with Maintenance first as always, and the Robot walks to the stockpile, loads them, which takes them out of the base's stock (or as much of each as is left), carries them to the Lander and puts them aboard. Its working time is set in the data catalogue alongside that for unloading cargo, with walking on top. The Work panel shows its step and progress, and a notification says when they are aboard. Until the Robot has loaded them, changing the amounts changes what it carries, and clearing them cancels the loading; once it has them, any change is refused until they are aboard, and so is sending the Lander. Once they are aboard, you can set more, which go aboard with them in another round of loading, the Return Mass counting what is aboard already. The take-off notification says what the Lander took, and the status panel shows the Exports aboard it until they are sold. When it reaches Earth, they are sold: the Funding rises by their value, and a notification says what was sold and for how much. That comes before the manifest is paid for as the Lander leaves Earth, so while Exports are aboard, the Launch manifest screen counts their value alongside the Funding balance, and accepts a manifest the balance alone can't pay for, as long as the two together can.

The status panel shows whether the base is self-sufficient in oxygen and in water, and the share of the Mission Director's use that the base's Plants made over the last Lunation. It is self-sufficient in a stock once, over that trailing window, the Plants made at least as much as the Mission Director used, judged every 6 game-hours on records of both (the data catalogue sets the window and the records). Because it looks back a whole Lunation, it stays self-sufficient through a Night when the Plants stand idle, or between Expeditions. It never is before a whole window has passed since the Campaign began (or, for a Campaign loaded from a save made before these records were kept, since it was loaded), or while the Mission Director lives aboard the Lander and uses nothing. Becoming self-sufficient, and ceasing to be, are notifications.

Add `?seed=<number>` to the address (for example <http://localhost:5173/?seed=42>) to replay a Campaign exactly; without it, every new Campaign gets a new seed.

"Save" in the top bar downloads the Campaign as a `.moonbase` file, named after the game clock (for example `moon-base-earth-day-3-0705.moonbase`), and "Load" opens a file picker and carries on from the file you choose, exactly as the saved Campaign would have, in place of the one you're playing. The game-over screen has both buttons too. Nothing is kept in the browser, so a Campaign you haven't saved is lost when you close or reload the page (ADR-0006). A file that isn't a Moon Base save, is damaged, or was saved by a newer version of the game is refused with the reason, and the Campaign you're playing carries on.

## Save files

A `.moonbase` file is JSON: `"format": "moonbase-save"`, the format `version`, the random generator's state (`random`) and the rest of the simulation state (`state`), with anything unset written as `null`. `src/simulation/save.ts` writes it and reads it back, checking every field. Each released format version is a compatibility contract: to change the format, raise the version by adding a migration from the one before to `migrations` in that file (never edit a released one), and check in a sample save of the new version beside the old ones in `test/saves/`, where every file must go on loading. Never edit or regenerate a sample. New Components change the format, as the base's stock keeps a count of every Component: the migration to the new version adds a count of none for each, after the others, naming them in the migration rather than reading them from the data catalogue (as the one to version 28 does for the large battery bank's), and each later Phase 7 Design that brings new Components adds a migration like it.

## Verification

`npm run verify` is the single verification command. Run it from the repository's root folder. It changes nothing apart from the ignored `dist/`, `test-results/` and `playwright-report/` folders, and it stops at the first failing check. CI runs the same command (`.github/workflows/verify.yml`) split across two parallel jobs: `npm run verify -- --skip-e2e` runs every check except the end-to-end tests, and `npm run verify -- --e2e-only` runs just those, so a failure in one job can be re-run on its own.

Before the first run on a machine, install Playwright's Firefox once:

```powershell
npx.cmd playwright install firefox
```

It runs these checks, in this order:

| #   | Check                                            | Script                             | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Formatting (Prettier)                            | `npm run format:check`             | `npm run format` fixes it.                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| 2   | Lint and static bug analysis (typescript-eslint) | `npm run lint`                     | Type-aware `strictTypeChecked` rules, `--max-warnings 0`. These rules are the static bug analysis gate; no separate analyzer is added.                                                                                                             |
| 3   | Type check (`tsc --noEmit`, strict)              | `npm run typecheck`                | Checks the whole project, then the simulation and data catalogue again with no DOM or Node types, so they stay headless (ADR-0003).                                                                                                                |
| 4   | Unit and scenario tests (Vitest)                 | `npm test`                         |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| 5   | End-to-end tests (Playwright, Firefox)           | `npm run test:e2e`                 | Starts the Vite dev server on port 4173. Firefox runs headless; set `PLAYWRIGHT_HEADED=1` to show its window, which CI does inside a virtual display because headless Firefox has no OpenGL without a GPU. Headed runs use one worker (see below). |
| 6   | Dependency audit and secret scan                 | `npm run audit`, `npm run secrets` | `npm audit` fails on any known vulnerability and needs network access. The secret scan uses secretlint's recommended rules.                                                                                                                        |
| 7   | Production build (Vite)                          | `npm run build`                    | Any bundler warning fails the build.                                                                                                                                                                                                               |

Every check applies; none is skipped. Tool versions are pinned exactly in `package.json` (`.npmrc` sets `save-exact`), and the Node version for CI is in `.nvmrc`.

### One worker for headed end-to-end runs

When Firefox runs headed (`PLAYWRIGHT_HEADED=1`, as in CI), `playwright.config.ts` runs the end-to-end tests one at a time. CI draws with Mesa's software renderer into one virtual display, and two Firefox windows there hold back each other's animation frames. The game updates what it shows only once a frame, so a test whose page gets no frames can't see anything change. With two workers, one test per run took about 20 s longer than usual, and sometimes long enough to time out. In the slowest case its page went 28 s without a frame while its timers ran on time. With one worker no page went more than 1.4 s without a frame, and the whole suite took as long as with two, because both workers compete for the same CPUs. Headless runs on a developer machine still use Playwright's default number of workers.

### Timeouts

A timeout is there to stop a test that hangs, not to check how fast it runs. Each limit is set well above the slowest run seen in CI, so ordinary variation in machine speed can't fail a test. If a test needs to check speed, it should measure it explicitly. Set limits for the whole suite, not for single tests.

| Limit                                 | Where                             | Value  | Slowest seen in CI                                                    | Margin    |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| Vitest test and hook timeout          | `vitest.config.ts`                | 60 s   | about 6 s (a long scenario test; CI runs Vitest about 2.5× slower)    | 10×       |
| Playwright test timeout               | `playwright.config.ts`, `timeout` | 120 s  | about 11 s (`construction.spec.ts` and `habitat.spec.ts`, one worker) | about 10× |
| Playwright `expect` and `expect.poll` | `playwright.config.ts`, `expect`  | 60 s   | about 8 s (a refusal that hides after a fixed 8 s)                    | 7.5×      |
| Playwright web server start           | Playwright's default              | 60 s   | under 1 s (Vite is ready in about 0.2 s)                              | over 60×  |
| Each CI job                           | `.github/workflows/verify.yml`    | 10 min | about 1.5 min                                                         | about 7×  |

The cost of a generous limit is that a test that really hangs takes longer to report. That is cheaper than a red run that has to be investigated and re-run.

### Exceptions

- **Vite chunk size limit raised to 700 kB** (`vite.config.ts`, `build.chunkSizeWarningLimit`). Three.js alone is about 530 kB minified, and the single-page game needs all of it before it can draw, so code-splitting would not make anything load sooner. Review if the bundle grows past the limit or the game gains screens that could load on demand.
- **`@typescript-eslint/restrict-template-expressions` allows numbers** (`eslint.config.js`, option `allowNumber: true`; every other operand type stays forbidden). Numbers always print sensibly in template strings, and the game builds many labels and messages from them (clock times, ids, refusal reasons), so wrapping each one in `String()` would add noise without catching any bug. Review if the rule's defaults change or a number-formatting bug turns up in a template string.

## Project layout

- `src/simulation/`: the headless, deterministic game rules. Create a Campaign from a seed, advance it in fixed 1-game-minute ticks, and read immutable snapshots. It never imports the renderer or interface, and never reads the system clock or `Math.random`; lint rules and a separate type check enforce this.
- `src/catalogue/`: the typed data catalogue holding all content and tuning numbers.
- `src/game/`: the game loop, which turns real time into simulation ticks at the chosen speed, and the Campaign slot and save files, which load a Campaign from a file in place of the one being played.
- `src/render/`: the Three.js renderer, which only reads snapshots and the data catalogue (for sizes and timings such as the Lander's footprint and the length of a Lunation, which it converts with the simulation's own `lunationMinutes`). It never changes simulation state.
- `src/ui/`: the HTML interface, which reads snapshots and sends player choices.
- `test/`: Vitest scenario tests through the simulation interface; `test/scenario.ts` is the helper for writing them.
- `e2e/`: Playwright end-to-end tests in Firefox.
- `.claude/launch.json`: tells the Claude desktop app how to start the dev server for its browser preview.
